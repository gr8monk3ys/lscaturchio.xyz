## A market that shouldn't work this well

Walk through Huaqiangbei in Shenzhen and you'll find stalls the size of a closet doing repairs no authorized Apple Store will attempt: reballing a water-damaged logic board, swapping a single failed NAND chip off a phone that won't boot, micro-soldering a torn trace back together under a microscope for the price of a nice lunch. None of this is hobbyist tinkering. It's a mature trade, with its own supply chains of salvaged donor boards, its own training pipelines, its own reputational economy of technicians who can fix what an authorized service center would simply replace.

It shouldn't be surprising that this exists. It's surprising how far outside the official picture of "repair" it sits.

## Two repair economies, one pretending the other doesn't exist

The right-to-repair fight in the US and Europe is mostly about access: can an independent shop or an owner buy the part, read the schematic, clear the software flag. [The last piece in this series](/blog/right-to-repair-civil-right) went through how manufacturers restrict all three, and how the FTC documented it plainly in *Nixing the Fix*. That fight assumes a baseline of technical capability. Give an independent US shop the parts and the diagnostic software, the thinking goes, and the repair itself isn't the hard part.

Component-level repair, the kind done in Shenzhen or at chip-level training academies in Delhi, doesn't wait for that permission. It routinely solves problems authorized channels don't even attempt: a single failed chip on a $1,000 logic board, instead of ordering a full board replacement that costs almost as much as the device. Louis Rossmann, an American repair shop owner and right-to-repair advocate, has said for years that he learned more from Shenzhen's board-level repair culture, and sources reclaimed components through it, than from anything Apple ever published. That's a strange admission for a system that's supposed to be technologically ahead: the frontier of practical repair skill developed where manufacturers had the least reach, not the most.

## Repair as an informal economy, not a charity case

It's tempting to frame all of this as e-waste dumping: rich countries' broken electronics shipped somewhere poorer to be picked apart. That framing isn't wrong, exactly, and [the environmental cost is real](/blog/right-to-repair-civil-right). But it flattens something that deserves more credit than "dumping ground."

Lagos's Alaba International Market and Ikeja Computer Village process enormous volumes of secondhand and supposedly dead electronics every year, and a large share of what arrives gets diagnosed, repaired, and resold, not landfilled. Technicians there do component-level board work on devices with no service manual, no manufacturer support, and no legal path to authorized parts, because none of that was ever offered to them in the first place. Delhi's Seelampur and Old Delhi markets run a similar trade at enormous scale: informal apprenticeship, salvaged parts, and enough accumulated skill that people bring in devices no formal repair chain in India would touch.

None of this runs on charity or desperation alone. It runs on demand: hundreds of millions of people for whom a $1,000 replacement device is not a rounding error, and for whom a $40 board-level repair is the difference between staying connected and not. The economics that make manufacturer-authorized repair unprofitable in wealthy markets (why fix a $279 screen when you can sell a $999 phone) make independent repair essential everywhere income is lower and devices have to last.

## Locked out at both ends

Here's the part that should unsettle the standard telling of the story. These are, by any measure, some of the most skilled repair technicians in the electronics industry. And manufacturers treat them the same way they treat a random stranger with a screwdriver: no schematics, no diagnostic access, no parts pairing exemption, no acknowledgment that this repair economy exists at all, let alone that it works.

[Server-dependent obsolescence](/blog/obsolescence-as-a-service) at least requires a company to actively flip a switch. This is quieter: a manufacturer just never builds a door for informal repair markets to walk through, and lets a few hundred million devices a year get fixed anyway, off the books, by people the company's own support documentation was never written for. The skill gets developed in spite of the manufacturer, not because of any pipeline the manufacturer built.

The asymmetry cuts both ways. A shop in Ohio fighting for the legal right to buy a genuine replacement part is fighting the same company that a technician in Lagos has already routed around entirely, by learning to build a working substitute out of salvage. One approach asks for permission. The other never got the option to ask.

## What the rich world's repair movement could learn

Right-to-repair advocacy in wealthy countries tends to fight for parity with the manufacturer: the same official parts, the same documentation, the same diagnostic tools independent shops in the US and EU are currently denied. That's a reasonable, winnable fight, and Massachusetts's ballot initiative and the EU's parts-and-repairability rules show it can work.

But it's worth noticing that the most resourceful repair economies on the planet didn't wait for parity to develop real capability. They built a parallel one, with salvage instead of official parts and apprenticeship instead of certification programs, and it's often more capable at the hardest repairs than the system asking for a seat at the manufacturer's table. If a legal right to repair ever arrives everywhere at once, it will be catching up to what Shenzhen, Lagos, and Delhi already figured out without it: that a broken board is a puzzle, not an invitation to buy new.

## The repair the industry doesn't put in its sustainability report

Every manufacturer sustainability report talks about "circularity" and "extending device lifespans." None of them credit the actual mechanism doing most of that extending: an informal, globally distributed, largely unrecognized repair workforce that keeps devices alive for years after the official warranty, official parts supply, and official interest in the device's survival have all run out.

That's not a footnote to the repair story. It might be the largest working example of it, and it's happening entirely outside the frameworks, legislation, and manufacturer goodwill this series has spent three pieces arguing over.
