## A defense built for photocopiers, stretched over data centers

Fair use was written for a world of excerpts: a book reviewer quoting three paragraphs, a documentary using ten seconds of news footage, a parody that needed just enough of the original to land the joke. The four-factor test courts still use — the purpose and character of the use, the nature of the copyrighted work, how much of it was used, and the effect on the market for the original — was built to weigh small, identifiable borrowings against small, identifiable harms.

Large language models broke that scale before anyone finished building it. Training a frontier model means ingesting not three paragraphs but something closer to the written output of human civilization: hundreds of millions of books, articles, and images, copied in full, processed, and compressed into billions of numerical weights. Whether that counts as fair use was an open question with no real precedent, because nothing before it had tried to copy everything at once and call the copying "transformative." Since late 2023, a stack of lawsuits has been trying to answer it, and the early rulings don't agree with each other so much as they carve the question into pieces.

## The docket

The New York Times sued OpenAI and Microsoft in December 2023, alleging the companies trained on millions of Times articles and that the resulting models could reproduce substantial chunks of paywalled reporting verbatim. A separate consolidated group of authors, including Sarah Silverman and Michael Chabon, sued OpenAI and Meta over similar claims; most of the subsidiary theories (DMCA violations, unjust enrichment) were dismissed through 2024, leaving the core copyright claims to be litigated on their merits. Getty Images sued Stability AI on both sides of the Atlantic over Stable Diffusion's training set. Universal Music, Concord, and ABKCO sued Anthropic over song lyrics reproduced by Claude. Programmers sued GitHub, Microsoft, and OpenAI over Copilot's use of public code repositories. None of these resolved the underlying question cleanly — but two rulings in the summer of 2025, from different courts, on different facts, came closest to actually applying the four-factor test to an LLM, and reading them side by side tells you more than either one alone.

## Two rulings, nine days apart

In *Bartz v. Anthropic*, Judge William Alsup in the Northern District of California split the case down the middle. Training a model on books Anthropic had legitimately purchased, he held, was "exceedingly transformative" and squarely protected fair use — the model wasn't storing or redistributing the books, it was learning statistical patterns from them, which is a different thing in kind from the market a book publisher serves. But Anthropic hadn't only used purchased books. It had also downloaded millions of titles from pirate libraries — shadow repositories built from unauthorized scans — to build out its training corpus faster and more cheaply than buying and scanning copies itself would have allowed. Alsup drew a hard line there: acquiring the books that way had nothing to do with training being transformative, and the piracy claim went to trial on damages alone.

Around the same time, Judge Vince Chhabria in the same district ruled for Meta in *Kadrey v. Meta* on a comparable fair-use question, but on narrower grounds and with a pointed caveat. The plaintiffs in front of him, he wrote, simply hadn't put in evidence that Meta's use of their specific books had harmed the market for those books — no showing of displaced sales, no market-dilution argument built out on the record. He was explicit that this wasn't a blanket ruling that training LLMs on copyrighted text is lawful; a future plaintiff with a better-built market-harm case could still win on the same general fact pattern. Two judges, two Meta-and-Anthropic-sized AI labs, both landing on fair use — but for reasons narrow enough that neither opinion functions as the clear rule the industry wanted.

Contrast both with *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence*, decided earlier that same year by Judge Stephanos Bibas in Delaware. Ross had trained a legal-research AI on Westlaw's editorially written headnotes — not to produce prose like Westlaw's, but to build a product that competed directly with Westlaw in the same market, for the same customers. Bibas rejected the fair use defense outright. The use wasn't meaningfully transformative, and more importantly, it served as a market substitute. Put the three rulings together and a pattern starts to show through the noise: courts are far more willing to call training transformative when the output doesn't directly compete with the thing it was trained on, and far less willing when it does. That's a real distinction, but it's also one that has to be relitigated, industry by industry and product by product, because nothing in the text of fair use doctrine draws it for them in advance.

## The actual price tag

The clearest number to come out of any of this isn't a legal holding at all — it's a settlement. In September 2025, Anthropic agreed to pay roughly $1.5 billion to resolve the piracy portion of the Bartz case: something on the order of 500,000 books, at roughly $3,000 per title, reportedly the largest publicly disclosed copyright recovery on record. It's worth being precise about what that settlement did and didn't decide. It didn't resolve whether training on copyrighted text is fair use — Alsup had already ruled that it was, for the legitimately acquired half of the corpus. What got priced was the acquisition method: downloading a library instead of buying it. That's a meaningfully narrower proposition than "AI training infringes copyright," but it's the one with an actual dollar figure attached, and every AI company that built a training set the same way now has a number to think about.

## Why no clean rule is coming

Part of why these cases keep splitting on narrow, fact-specific grounds is that the four-factor test was never designed to produce a single bright-line answer, and American copyright law has no equivalent of the European approach. The EU's 2019 Copyright Directive created a text-and-data-mining exception that lets AI developers scrape copyrighted material by default, but lets rightsholders opt out — and the EU AI Act, which entered into force in 2024, requires general-purpose model providers to respect those opt-outs and publish a "sufficiently detailed summary" of what they trained on. It's not a settled framework either, and enforcement is still shaking out, but it at least names the transparency and opt-out questions directly instead of leaving them to emerge case by case from litigation. The American path runs entirely through federal courts weighing competing experts on market substitution, one lawsuit and one industry at a time — music publishers, news organizations, legal-research companies, code repositories, each getting their own trial on facts that don't transfer cleanly to the next one.

That case-by-case grind matters beyond the courtroom, because it's downstream of the same economic stakes covered in [the piece on automation and jobs](/blog/future-of-work) on this site: companies are racing to deploy models trained on exactly this contested corpus into the workplace, well ahead of any court settling whether the training itself was lawful. And the fair-use framing itself has the same blind spot covered in [the piece on AI art and process](/blog/ai-art-death-of-process) — these cases are being argued almost entirely as a question of market substitution and transformation, which is a legal question about outputs, while the actual objection from a lot of writers, musicians, and programmers is about the unpaid, uncredited use of their labor as raw material. A court can rule that a model's output doesn't compete with a novelist's backlist and be legally correct, without that ruling touching the separate claim that the novelist's work was taken and compensated for by no one.

## Where this leaves things

Nothing about the current run of rulings should be read as "AI training is legal" or "AI training is illegal." What exists right now is a handful of fact-specific holdings that happen to point the same general direction — transformation can satisfy fair use, but market substitution and piracy can defeat it even when transformation is present — decided by different judges, on different records, with the biggest single number generated by a settlement over how a corpus was acquired rather than a ruling on whether training itself infringes. The New York Times case is still working through discovery. The Getty litigation hasn't produced a clean U.S. ruling. The music publishers' case against Anthropic is still live. Every one of those will add another data point to a test that was built to weigh short quotations, and is now being asked to weigh the training process of models that read more in an afternoon than any one plaintiff's lawyers will read in the course of the case.
